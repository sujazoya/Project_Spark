Circuit
      Measurement: 
A circuit can be examined by measuring voltage between two points, current through a branch, and resistance when the circuit is de-energized and isolated as required. Use a voltmeter or multimeter in voltage mode in parallel with the points being tested. Measure current with an ammeter in series with the branch. Before measuring resistance or continuity, disconnect the power and ensure stored energy is safely discharged. Compare measurements with the expected circuit behavior to identify open paths, incorrect connections, or unexpected voltage drops.

    ohms_law:
      Id: 
ohms_law
      Title: 
Ohm's Law
      Category: 
Electricity
      Measurement: 
Ohm's law relates voltage, current, and resistance.

Main formula:
V = I × R

Calculate current:
I = V / R

Calculate resistance:
R = V / I

Example:
A 12 V source is connected across a 6 Ω resistor.

I = V / R
I = 12 / 6
I = 2 A

The current is 2 amperes.

V = voltage in volts (V)
I = current in amperes (A)
R = resistance in ohms (Ω)

For a fixed resistance, increasing voltage increases current.
For a fixed voltage, increasing resistance decreases current.

This relationship applies to ohmic components when their resistance remains approximately constant. Not all electronic components have a linear voltage-current relationship.

      Definition: 
Ohm’s law states that the current through an ohmic conductor is equal to the voltage across it divided by its resistance, provided its physical conditions remain constant.
      Explanation: 
Ohm’s law relates voltage, current, and resistance in a circuit. Its formula is:

V=I×R

      Misconception: 
Ohm’s law does not mean every component has constant resistance. Incandescent lamps, diodes, LEDs, and many other components can have nonlinear voltage-current behavior. Use the relationship with the appropriate component model and operating conditions.
      Safety: 
Use Ohm’s law to plan low-voltage training circuits and check component ratings. Always consider resistor power dissipation using P=VI and never use calculations as a reason to experiment with mains electricity.
